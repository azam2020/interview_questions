AMD VLSI Interview Q&A

Technical Reference based on AMD_AnkitRai_VLSI.pdf[cite: 1]

Round 1: Analog and Device Physics

Q: Draw the CMOS inverter circuit diagram, label all its terminals, and describe the functionality of the inverter.[cite: 1]
Answer: A CMOS inverter consists of a PMOS transistor on top and an NMOS transistor on the bottom.
  • Terminals: VDD (connected to PMOS source), GND (connected to NMOS source), Vin (connected to both PMOS and NMOS gates), and Vout (connected to the drain of both transistors).
  • Functionality: It acts as a logic NOT gate. When Vin is HIGH (VDD), the NMOS turns ON and the PMOS turns OFF, pulling Vout to GND (Logic 0). When Vin is LOW (GND), the PMOS turns ON and the NMOS turns OFF, pulling Vout to VDD (Logic 1).
Q: Where are the body terminals of the PMOS and NMOS connected? Why are they connected to VDD and GND respectively?[cite: 1]
Answer: The body of a PMOS is connected to the highest potential in the circuit (VDD), and the body of an NMOS is connected to the lowest potential (GND). This is done to keep the parasitic PN junctions (formed between the Source/Drain diffusion regions and the body/substrate) strictly reverse-biased. If these junctions were forward-biased, it would cause massive leakage current and potentially trigger latch-up, destroying the device.
Q: What happens if the PMOS body is connected to GND and the NMOS body is connected to VDD?[cite: 1]
Answer: The PN junctions between the Source/Drain and the substrate will become heavily forward-biased. This creates a direct low-resistance path (short circuit) between the power supply and ground, resulting in enormous current flow that will permanently damage or melt the silicon chip. The logic functionality will completely fail.
Q: What occurs if the PMOS body and NMOS body are connected to optimum values (neither VDD nor GND)?[cite: 1]
Answer: Connecting the body to intermediate voltages introduces the Body Effect. If we apply a reverse body bias (e.g., NMOS body < GND, PMOS body > VDD), the threshold voltage (Vth) increases, which slows down the transistor but significantly reduces subthreshold leakage power. If we apply a forward body bias (e.g., NMOS body > GND but less than turn-on voltage), Vth decreases, making the transistor switch faster at the cost of higher leakage.
Q: Draw the cross-sectional diagram of a PMOS transistor and explain all its modes of operation.[cite: 1]
Answer: A PMOS is built inside an N-well placed in a P-substrate. It has heavily doped P+ regions for the Source and Drain, and an N+ contact for the N-well body terminal. Above the channel is the thin oxide and the gate terminal.
  • Cut-off: VGS > VTH (where VTH is negative). The channel is not formed; no current flows.
  • Linear (Triode): VGS < VTH and VDS > VGS - VTH. The channel acts as a continuous resistor. Current is proportional to VDS.
  • Saturation: VGS < VTH and VDS < VGS - VTH. The channel pinches off near the drain. Current is largely independent of VDS and depends quadratically on VGS.
Q: How do you calculate the input voltage given the VDS and VTH for a PMOS transistor?[cite: 1]
Answer: The state of the transistor (Linear vs Saturation) dictates the relationship. However, the boundary between saturation and linear regions is defined by VDS = VGS - VTH. If operating on the edge of saturation, VGS = VDS + VTH. Since the source is usually connected to VDD, the input voltage at the gate (VG) can be found using VGS = VG - VS. Thus, VG = VGS + VDD.
Q: What happens if NMOS and PMOS transistors are swapped in an inverter?[cite: 1]
Answer: It functions as a very poor voltage buffer rather than an inverter. NMOS is poor at passing a logic 1 (it passes VDD - Vthn), and PMOS is poor at passing a logic 0 (it passes |Vthp|). If the input is 1, the NMOS turns on and pulls the output up to a degraded 1 (VDD - Vth). If the input is 0, the PMOS turns on and pulls the output down to a degraded 0 (|Vthp|).

Round 2: Digital and Physical Design

Q: Explain the complete Physical design flow in detail.[cite: 1]
Answer: The physical design (RTL-to-GDSII) flow consists of several stages:
  1. Design Import / Floorplanning: Partitioning the chip, placing I/O pads, macros, and determining the core area.
  2. Power Planning: Creating the power grid (VDD/GND rings, stripes, and rails) to ensure uniform IR-drop and power distribution.
  3. Placement: Placing the standard cells optimally to minimize wire length and congestion without causing overlaps.
  4. Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS): Routing the clock signal to all sequential elements to minimize skew and latency.
  5. Routing: Connecting all the placed components with metal layers (Global and Detailed routing).
  6. Sign-off: Physical verification (DRC, LVS), Timing sign-off (STA), and Power analysis before sending the final GDSII file to the foundry.
Q: What do you mean by Clock Tree Synthesis?[cite: 1]
Answer: Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS) is the process of distributing the clock signal from a clock source to all the sequential elements (like flip-flops) in the design. The primary goal is to ensure that the clock reaches all elements simultaneously, minimizing clock skew and keeping insertion delay (latency) within acceptable limits, while adhering to DRC rules.
Q: What is STA, and at which stage of physical design is STA performed?[cite: 1]
Answer: Static Timing Analysis (STA) is a mathematical method of verifying the expected timing behavior (setup and hold) of a digital circuit without simulating logical functionality. STA is performed at multiple stages: Pre-layout (post-synthesis), Post-placement, Post-CTS, and Post-routing (Sign-off).
Q: What are setup time and hold time, and why is it important to check them?[cite: 1]
Answer:
  • Setup Time: The minimum amount of time data must be stable before the active edge of the clock.
  • Hold Time: The minimum amount of time data must remain stable after the active edge of the clock.
Checking them is critical because if data changes within these windows, the flip-flop may capture incorrect data or enter a metastable state, causing system failure.
Q: What is metastability, where is it most likely to occur, and how can it be mitigated?[cite: 1]
Answer: Metastability is a state where a flip-flop's output hovers at an indeterminate voltage level (neither 0 nor 1) before eventually settling randomly. It occurs when setup or hold times are violated, most commonly in Cross-Clock Domain (CDC) crossings or when capturing asynchronous external inputs. It is mitigated by using multi-stage synchronizers (like a 2-flop synchronizer) to give the signal time to settle before entering the main logic.
Q: What methods are used to reduce setup and hold violations?[cite: 1]
Answer:
  • Setup Violations: Reduce combinational delay (upsize cells, use lower Vth cells, logic restructuring), increase the clock period (lower frequency), or utilize positive clock skew.
  • Hold Violations: Increase combinational delay (insert buffers/delay cells in the data path), downsize driving cells, use higher Vth cells, or utilize negative clock skew.
Q: What are clock skew and jitter, and what methods are used to reduce them?[cite: 1]
Answer:
  • Clock Skew: The spatial variation in clock arrival time at different flip-flops. Reduced by better CTS topologies (H-trees, meshes) and clock buffer balancing.
  • Clock Jitter: The temporal (time-based) variation of the clock period from cycle to cycle. Reduced by using highly stable PLLs, providing clean power supplies to the clock generators, and proper shielding of clock nets.
Q: Write the equations for setup time and hold time. What happens if a delay is added in the clock path of the capture flip-flop (Positive Skew)?[cite: 1]
Answer:
  • Setup Equation: Tcq + Tcomb + Tsetup ≤ Tclk + Tskew
  • Hold Equation: Tcq + Tcomb ≥ Thold + Tskew
Adding delay to the capture clock path creates Positive Skew. This relaxes the setup time requirement, allowing the circuit to run at a higher speed (smaller Tclk). However, it severely degrades the hold margin, making the circuit susceptible to hold violations which can cause immediate functional failure.
Q: Draw the CMOS circuit diagram of a 3-input NOR gate.[cite: 1]
Answer: A 3-input NOR gate consists of a pull-up network and a pull-down network. The pull-up network has three PMOS transistors connected in series between VDD and the output node. The pull-down network has three NMOS transistors connected in parallel between the output node and GND. Each input (A, B, C) drives one PMOS and one NMOS pair.
Q: Explain the regions of operation for both NMOS and PMOS transistors in a complete VTC curve.[cite: 1]
Answer: In the Voltage Transfer Characteristic (VTC) curve of a CMOS inverter:
  • Region A (Vin = 0): NMOS is Cutoff, PMOS is Linear. Output = VDD.
  • Region B: NMOS is Saturation, PMOS is Linear. Output is High but falling.
  • Region C (Transition point): Both NMOS and PMOS are in Saturation. Output drops sharply. Short circuit current is maximum.
  • Region D: NMOS is Linear, PMOS is Saturation. Output is Low but falling slowly to GND.
  • Region E (Vin = VDD): NMOS is Linear, PMOS is Cutoff. Output = GND.
Q: Explain Noise Margin, 2nd-order effects (Body Effect, CLM, Subthreshold leakage, Temp effect), Latch-up, Electromigration, and Antenna Effect.[cite: 1]
Answer:
  • Noise Margin: The maximum noise voltage that can be added to an input signal without causing the output to change logical state incorrectly.
  • Body Effect: The change in transistor threshold voltage (Vth) due to a voltage difference between the source and the body.
  • Channel Length Modulation (CLM): In saturation, as VDS increases, the effective channel length decreases, causing drain current to slightly increase rather than remain perfectly constant.
  • Subthreshold Leakage: Current that flows between source and drain even when VGS < Vth. Minimized by multi-Vt libraries and power gating.
  • Temp Effect on Vth: As temperature increases, Vth decreases (lowering threshold margin), but carrier mobility also decreases.
  • Latch-up: The unintended creation of a low-impedance path between power supply rails caused by parasitic BJTs forming a thyristor. Prevented using guard rings and tap cells.
  • Electromigration: The gradual displacement of metal atoms in interconnects caused by high current densities (electron wind), leading to voids or hillocks. Prevented by using wider metal lines and avoiding 90-degree corners.
  • Antenna Effect: Charge accumulation on floating metal interconnects during plasma etching, which can break down the thin gate oxide. Prevented by using antenna diodes or metal jumping (routing on higher layers).
Q: Which is more critical, a setup violation or a hold violation, and how can a setup violation be resolved after chip fabrication?[cite: 1]
Answer: A Hold Violation is much more critical because it is frequency-independent; if a hold violation exists, the chip will fail at any clock speed. A Setup Violation can be resolved after fabrication by simply reducing the clock frequency (running the chip slower) or by slightly increasing the operating voltage (VDD) to speed up the transistors.
Q: What is temperature inversion, and in what way does temperature influence the reduction of setup and hold violations after fabrication?[cite: 1]
Answer: Temperature Inversion is a phenomenon observed in advanced technology nodes (sub-65nm). Traditionally, higher temperatures slow down transistors due to reduced mobility. However, at lower VDDs in advanced nodes, the decrease in threshold voltage (Vth) at higher temperatures dominates the mobility degradation, making the transistor actually faster at higher temperatures. Consequently, worst-case setup delays might happen at cold temperatures, and worst-case hold violations might happen at hot temperatures, completely inverting traditional timing analysis logic. Adjusting operating temperature post-fabrication can thus dynamically impact setup/hold margins depending on the VDD and node size.
