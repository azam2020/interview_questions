AMD VLSI & CS Interview Prep Guide

1. Device Physics & CMOS Basics
Q: What is a MOSFET? What is Threshold Voltage? How can you vary it?
A MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) is a semiconductor device used to switch or amplify electrical signals. Threshold voltage (Vth) is the minimum gate-to-source voltage needed to create a conducting channel between the source and drain. It can be varied using body biasing (changing the bulk voltage), changing the oxide thickness, or altering the channel doping concentration.
Q: Explain the different regions of operation in a MOSFET.
1. Cutoff: Vgs < Vth. No channel exists; transistor is OFF.
2. Linear (Triode): Vgs > Vth and Vds < Vgs - Vth. Acts as a voltage-controlled resistor.
3. Saturation: Vgs > Vth and Vds ≥ Vgs - Vth. Current becomes nearly constant; used for amplification and as a closed switch.
Q: Where are the body terminals of PMOS and NMOS connected and why? What if they are swapped?
The PMOS body is connected to VDD (highest potential) and the NMOS body to GND (lowest potential) to ensure the parasitic PN junctions between the substrate and the source/drain remain reverse-biased. If swapped, these junctions become forward-biased, causing massive short-circuit currents leading to a destructive condition known as Latch-up.
Q: What is Channel Length Modulation (CLM)?
As Vds increases beyond saturation (Vds > Vgs - Vth), the effective channel length decreases because the pinch-off point moves towards the source. This causes the drain current to increase slightly rather than staying perfectly constant. It affects the output resistance of the transistor.
Q: Explain Latch-up, Electromigration, and Antenna Effect. How are they prevented?
  • Latch-up: The creation of a low-impedance path between VDD and GND due to parasitic BJTs turning on. Prevention: Tap cells, guard rings.
  • Electromigration: The gradual displacement of metal atoms in a conductor due to high current density, causing opens or shorts. Prevention: Widening metal traces, reducing current density, using via arrays.
  • Antenna Effect: Charge accumulation on long metal interconnects during plasma etching, which can damage the thin gate oxide. Prevention: Inserting jumper metals (routing to a higher metal layer) or placing reverse-biased antenna diodes.
Q: What is Noise Margin?
Noise margin is the maximum amount of noise (voltage spike or drop) that can be added to an input signal without causing the gate to output an incorrect logic level. It guarantees the reliability of digital circuits.
2. Digital Logic & Static Timing Analysis (STA)
Q: What is STA? Why and where is it performed?
Static Timing Analysis is a method to validate the timing performance of a design by checking all paths for timing violations (Setup and Hold) without simulating the dynamic behavior. It is performed at multiple stages: after logic synthesis, after placement, and after routing (post-CTS).
Q: What are Setup and Hold time? Why are they important?
Setup Time: The minimum time the data must be stable before the active clock edge. Hold Time: The minimum time the data must be stable after the active clock edge. They ensure the flip-flop reliably captures the correct data without entering metastability.
Q: How do you fix Setup and Hold violations? Which is more critical?
Setup fixes: Upsize gates (drive strength), use Low-Vth cells, reduce combinational logic depth, logic restructuring.
Hold fixes: Add buffers/delay cells in the data path, downsize gates.
Criticality: Hold violations are more critical because they are independent of clock frequency; if a hold violation exists, the chip will fail regardless of how much you slow down the clock.
Q: What is Metastability?
If setup or hold times are violated, the flip-flop's internal nodes may hover at an intermediate voltage level (neither 0 nor 1) for an unpredictable amount of time before resolving. This can corrupt downstream logic. Mitigated using multi-stage synchronizers.
Q: Explain Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS), Skew, and Jitter.
CTS: The process of distributing the clock signal to all sequential elements while minimizing skew and insertion delay. Skew: The spatial variation in clock arrival time at different flip-flops. (Local skew = between adjacent FFs; Global skew = max difference across the entire chip). Jitter: The temporal (time-based) variation of the clock period from its ideal value.
Q: Setup and Hold Equations with Clock Skew (FF1 to FF2)
Setup: T_clk ≥ T_cq + T_comb + T_setup - T_skew (Positive skew helps setup time).
Hold: T_cq + T_comb ≥ T_hold + T_skew (Positive skew degrades hold time).
Q: What is Latch / Time Borrowing?
Because latches are level-sensitive (transparent for half the clock cycle), if data arrives slightly late, it can still pass through during the transparent window. This essentially "borrows" time from the next logic stage, offering flexibility in timing closure compared to strict edge-triggered flip-flops.
3. Verilog & Digital Design
Q: Write Verilog code for a D flip-flop with an asynchronous active-low reset.
module dff (input clk, input rst_n, input d, output reg q);
    always @(posedge clk or negedge rst_n) begin
        if (!rst_n)
            q <= 1'b0;
        else
            q <= d;
    end
endmodule
Q: Difference between Synchronous and Asynchronous Reset (Synthesis impact)?
Asynchronous: Resets immediately regardless of the clock. Synthesizes to the dedicated reset pin of a flip-flop. (Included in the sensitivity list).
Synchronous: Resets only on the active clock edge. Synthesizes as combinational logic (a mux) feeding into the D-pin of the flip-flop.
Q: FSM: Mealy vs Moore Machines
Moore: Output depends ONLY on the current state. Safer, but generally requires more states. Mealy: Output depends on current state AND current inputs. Reacts faster (asynchronous outputs), requires fewer states, but can cause glitches if inputs change mid-cycle.
4. Puzzles
Q: 4 people (A=1m, B=2m, C=7m, D=10m) cross a bridge at night with 1 torch. Max 2 at a time. Minimum time?
17 minutes.
1. A and B cross (2 mins).
2. A returns with torch (1 min).
3. C and D cross (10 mins).
4. B returns with torch (2 mins).
5. A and B cross (2 mins).
Total: 2 + 1 + 10 + 2 + 2 = 17 mins.
Q: 1000 bottles, 1 is poisonous. Find it using 10 rats.
Use binary representation. Label the bottles 1 to 1000. 10 rats represent the 10 bit positions (2^10 = 1024, which is > 1000). For each bottle, convert its number to binary. If the nth bit is 1, feed a drop from that bottle to the nth rat. After the poison takes effect, the dead rats will represent the binary bits that are '1'. Convert that binary number back to decimal to find the exact poisonous bottle.
