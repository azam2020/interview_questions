Round 1: Verilog & Physical Design

Q: What is hardware modelling using Verilog?[cite: 4]
Answer: Hardware modeling is the process of describing the architecture, behavior, and structural logic of a digital system using a Hardware Description Language (HDL) like Verilog. It allows engineers to design at various levels of abstraction (behavioral, dataflow, gate-level, and switch-level) so the design can be simulated for functional correctness and later synthesized into actual physical logic gates.
Q: How do you infer latches in Verilog, and why is it usually considered a mistake in FPGA implementation?[cite: 4]
Answer: Latches are inferred unintentionally in combinational blocks (always @* or always_comb) when a variable is not assigned a value in all possible execution paths (e.g., missing an else branch in an if statement, or a default case in a case statement).

Why it's a mistake: FPGAs are primarily flip-flop based. Unintentional latches cause severe static timing analysis (STA) issues, make the design difficult to test, and can lead to unpredictable glitches since latches are level-sensitive and transparent to combinational hazards.
Q: Explain Frequency Division concepts.[cite: 4]
Answer: Frequency division reduces a high-frequency clock to a lower-frequency clock.
  • Divide-by-2 ($f/2$): Implemented using a simple D-Flip-Flop with its inverted output ($\overline{Q}$) fed back into its D input.
  • Divide-by-$N$ (Even): Achieved using a synchronous counter that counts from $0$ to $(N/2)-1$ and toggles the output clock on overflow.
  • Divide-by-$N$ (Odd): Requires more complex logic, utilizing both positive and negative edges of the source clock to achieve a precise 50% duty cycle.

Round 2: CMOS Logic & Analog Circuits

Q: Draw an inverter using NAND gates. There are two designs; which should be preferred?[cite: 4]
Answer:
  • Design 1: Tie both inputs of a 2-input NAND together (Input A to both pins).
  • Design 2: Connect one input to A, and tie the other input to Logic 1 ($V_{DD}$).
Preferred: Design 2 is strictly better in physical design. In Design 1, the driving signal must drive the gate capacitance of both input transistors, doubling the input load and slowing down the previous stage. In Design 2, the driving signal only charges one gate capacitance, resulting in a faster and less power-hungry circuit.
Q: Design an XOR using 2-input NAND gates.[cite: 4]
Answer: The most optimized way requires 4 NAND gates:
  1. NAND1: NAND(A, B)
  2. NAND2: NAND(A, NAND1)
  3. NAND3: NAND(B, NAND1)
  4. NAND4: NAND(NAND2, NAND3) $\rightarrow A \oplus B$
Q: Is there any significance to putting one of the inputs closer to the output in a NOR CMOS implementation?[cite: 4]
Answer: Yes, this relies on transistor ordering (or pin reassigning). In a NOR gate, the PMOS transistors are in series. The critical/late-arriving signal should be assigned to the PMOS transistor closest to the output node.
Reason: If the late signal is at the top (closest to $V_{DD}$), it has to charge intermediate parasitic node capacitances between the series transistors before reaching the output, increasing delay. Putting the late-arriving signal closest to the output discharges/charges the output node almost immediately, minimizing path delay.
Q: Which should be preferred, NAND or NOR?[cite: 4]
Answer: NAND is always preferred in CMOS design. Because electrons (NMOS) have higher mobility than holes (PMOS), PMOS transistors must be sized larger to achieve the same current. In a NAND gate, the inherently weak PMOS transistors are placed in parallel (which reduces resistance), while the strong NMOS transistors are in series. In a NOR gate, the weak PMOS are in series, creating a huge resistance stack that requires massive sizing to overcome, increasing area and parasitic capacitance.
Q: Draw a source follower circuit and its small signal model. Why is it called a source follower?[cite: 4]
Answer:
  • Circuit: Also known as a Common-Drain amplifier. The input is applied to the Gate, the Drain is tied directly to $V_{DD}$, and the output is taken from the Source (with a load resistor or current source to ground).
  • Small Signal Model: $V_{in}$ is between Gate and Ground. A dependent current source ($g_m \cdot V_{gs}$) points from Drain to Source in parallel with output resistance $r_o$.
  • Why the name: Because the voltage gain is approximately 1 ($A_v \approx 1$). If the gate voltage increases, the source voltage increases by almost exactly the same amount to maintain $V_{gs}$ slightly above $V_{th}$. The source literally "follows" the gate. It is used as a voltage buffer due to its high input impedance and low output impedance.
Q: If a fabricated chip has a hold violation, how would you fix it using temperature or VDD?[cite: 4]
Answer: Post-fabrication, hold violations cannot be fixed by lowering clock frequency. However, you can manipulate operating conditions[cite: 4]:
Temperature Inversion: In advanced deep sub-micron nodes (sub-65nm) operating at low VDD, higher temperatures actually cause the threshold voltage ($V_{th}$) to drop significantly, which overtakes the mobility degradation. Thus, the chip becomes faster at high temperatures. To fix a hold violation (which requires slowing down the data path), you would operate the chip at a colder temperature to increase cell delay, adding artificial hold margin.
Q: If a fabricated chip has a setup violation, without altering clock frequency, how would you fix it?[cite: 4]
Answer: Setup violations mean the data is arriving too late. You need to speed up the combinational logic. This can be done by increasing the operating voltage ($V_{DD}$) (often called overvolting). A higher $V_{DD}$ increases the drive current of the transistors, decreasing the propagation delay ($T_{comb}$) and allowing the data to meet the setup window[cite: 4].

Round 3: Advanced Concepts & Scripting

Q: Write Verilog code for an active-low asynchronous reset flip-flop.[cite: 4]
module async_rst_ff (
    input clk,
    input rst_n,
    input d,
    output reg q
);
    always @(posedge clk or negedge rst_n) begin
        if (!rst_n)
            q <= 1'b0;
        else
            q <= d;
    end
endmodule
Q: Write Verilog code for an active-low asynchronous reset latch.[cite: 4]
module async_rst_latch (
    input en,
    input rst_n,
    input d,
    output reg q
);
    always @(en or rst_n or d) begin
        if (!rst_n)
            q <= 1'b0;
        else if (en)
            q <= d;
    end
endmodule
Q: There are two paths: one with $n$ identical inverters, the other with $n$ identical buffers. Effect on duty cycle?[cite: 4]
Answer: The Inverter chain is much better for duty cycle.
  • Buffers: A buffer consists of two inverters. If the pull-up network is weaker than the pull-down network, the rise time is slower than the fall time. As a signal passes through $n$ buffers, this asymmetric delay accumulates in the same direction, severely shrinking or expanding the pulse width, causing massive Duty Cycle Distortion (DCD).
  • Inverters: An inverter flips the signal. A slow rise time on stage 1 becomes a slow fall time on stage 2. The asymmetry cancels itself out at every alternate stage, preserving the original 50% duty cycle much more effectively.
Q: If metastability time is more than one clock cycle, what should we do? (Regarding Synchronizers)[cite: 4]
Answer: A standard 2-flop synchronizer grants exactly one clock cycle of resolution time for the metastable signal to settle. If the Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) calculations show that the signal might remain metastable for longer than one cycle (usually in very high-frequency domains), you must use a 3-flop or N-flop synchronizer. Adding more flip-flops in series adds full clock cycles to the resolution budget, exponentially increasing the MTBF.
Q: Puzzle: If we have a 3L jug and a 5L jug, how will you measure 4L?[cite: 4]
Answer:
  1. Fill the 5L jug completely.
  2. Pour water from the 5L jug into the 3L jug until the 3L jug is full. (You now have exactly 2L left in the 5L jug).
  3. Empty the 3L jug entirely.
  4. Pour the remaining 2L from the 5L jug into the empty 3L jug. (The 3L jug now has 2L, with room for 1L more).
  5. Refill the 5L jug completely.
  6. Carefully pour from the 5L jug into the 3L jug until the 3L jug is full (This takes exactly 1L).
  7. Result: The 5L jug now contains exactly 4L ($5L - 1L = 4L$).
