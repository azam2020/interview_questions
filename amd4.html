AMD VLSI Interview Q&A

Technical Reference

Round 1: Transistor Level Design & Logic

Q: What is VLSI, and why is there so much demand for it?
Answer: VLSI stands for Very Large Scale Integration. It is the process of creating an integrated circuit (IC) by combining millions or billions of MOS transistors onto a single silicon chip . The demand is driven by the continuous need for miniaturization, higher processing power, and lower power consumption in advanced electronics like AI accelerators, smartphones, IoT devices, and automotive systems .
Q: What are the different Transistors used in the VLSI Industry? Why move to NMOS/PMOS compared to BJT?
Answer:
  • The primary transistors used are NMOS and PMOS, which together form CMOS (Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor) logic .
  • Why over BJT: BJTs (Bipolar Junction Transistors) are current-driven and dissipate static power constantly when turned on. MOSFETs are voltage-driven, offering virtually zero static power consumption (ideal for packing billions of transistors), better scalability to nanometer nodes, higher input impedance, and improved noise margins .
Q: How do you identify the Drain and Source terminals in NMOS/PMOS?
Answer: Structurally, the Source and Drain are completely symmetric. The identification depends entirely on the applied voltages:
  • NMOS: The terminal at the lower electrical potential acts as the Source (providing electrons).
  • PMOS: The terminal at the higher electrical potential acts as the Source (providing holes).
Q: Explain the CMOS Inverter, its truth table, and how it is derived.
Answer: A CMOS Inverter has a PMOS pull-up network connected to $V_{DD}$ and an NMOS pull-down network connected to GND . Both gates are tied together as the input ($V_{in}$) .

Truth Table & Derivation:
  • If $V_{in} = 0$ (Logic 0): PMOS turns ON, NMOS turns OFF. The output node is pulled up to $V_{DD}$ (Logic 1) .
  • If $V_{in} = 1$ (Logic 1): NMOS turns ON, PMOS turns OFF. The output node is pulled down to GND (Logic 0) .
Q: Is there any point of time in a CMOS Inverter when both transistors turn ON? What is the issue with that?
Answer: Yes, during the transition phase when the input voltage switches between 0 and 1 (specifically around $V_{in} = V_{DD}/2$), both the NMOS and PMOS operate in the saturation region .
The Issue: This creates a direct conductive path from $V_{DD}$ to Ground, resulting in Short-Circuit Power Dissipation. This wastes energy and can cause thermal issues if input transition times (slew rates) are excessively slow .
Q: What happens if $V_{GS} = 0$? What currents flow in NMOS/PMOS? (Subthreshold & Gate Leakage)
Answer: When $V_{GS} = 0$, the transistor is in the cut-off region and ideally no current should flow . However, in deep sub-micron nodes, two primary leakage currents occur:
  • Subthreshold Leakage: A small diffusion current flows from Drain to Source through the channel because the transistor doesn't turn off completely abruptly .
  • Gate Leakage: Electrons tunnel vertically through the ultra-thin $SiO_2$ gate oxide directly from the gate to the substrate/channel, governed by Quantum Mechanical tunneling .
Q: What are Universal Gates and why are they called so?
Answer: NAND and NOR are Universal Gates. They are called this because any fundamental logic operation (AND, OR, NOT) and consequently any complex boolean digital circuit can be implemented using only NAND gates or only NOR gates .
Q: Design an OR gate using NAND Gates.
Answer: By De Morgan's Law, $A + B = \overline{\overline{A} \cdot \overline{B}}$. We need three 2-input NAND gates:
  1. Invert A: Tie both inputs of NAND1 to A $\rightarrow \overline{A}$
  2. Invert B: Tie both inputs of NAND2 to B $\rightarrow \overline{B}$
  3. NAND the results: Feed $\overline{A}$ and $\overline{B}$ into NAND3 $\rightarrow \overline{\overline{A} \cdot \overline{B}} = A + B$.
Q: Design an OR gate using a 2:1 MUX.
Answer: A 2:1 MUX follows the equation: $Out = \overline{S}I_0 + S I_1$. To make an OR gate ($A + B$):
  • Connect A to the Select line ($S = A$).
  • Connect B to $I_0$.
  • Connect Logic 1 ($V_{DD}$) to $I_1$.
Equation becomes: $Out = \overline{A}B + A(1) = \overline{A}B + A$. By Boolean simplification ($A + \overline{A}B = A + B$), this functions as an OR gate.
Q: Jug Puzzle - I have a 2L jug and a 4L jug, how will you measure 3L?
Answer: It is mathematically impossible to measure an odd amount (3L) using only even-capacity jugs (2L and 4L). Any combination of adding and subtracting 2 and 4 will always result in an even number (greatest common divisor of 2 and 4 is 2, and 3 is not divisible by 2).
Q: Jug Puzzle - I have a 2L jug and a 3L jug, now measure 4L?
Answer: Since a 3L jug and a 2L jug cannot independently hold 4L, you need a larger external vessel (or you just accumulate 4L). Logic: Fill the 2L jug and pour it into the large vessel. Fill the 2L jug again and pour it into the vessel. Total measured = $2L + 2L = 4L$.

Round 2: STA & Physical Design Logic

Q: Draw the Setup and Hold time equations in STA. How will you find the maximum frequency?
Answer:
  • Setup Time Equation: $T_{cq} + T_{comb\_max} + T_{setup} \le T_{clk} + T_{skew}$
  • Hold Time Equation: $T_{cq} + T_{comb\_min} \ge T_{hold} + T_{skew}$
  • Maximum Frequency ($F_{max}$): Derived from the setup equation (assuming zero skew for ideal case): $$T_{clk\_min} = T_{cq} + T_{comb\_max} + T_{setup}$$ $$F_{max} = \frac{1}{T_{clk\_min}}$$
Q: What is a False Path in STA?
Answer: A False Path is a physical timing path present in the netlist that exists structurally but can never be logically sensitized or activated during actual functional operation . Examples include paths crossed by mutually exclusive logic or static configuration registers. These must be explicitly constrained (ignored) in STA so the tool doesn't waste time optimizing a path that never switches.
Q: What is Time Borrowing? Why does it happen in Latches and not Flip-flops?
Answer:
  • Time Borrowing (Cycle Stealing): A technique where a slow logic stage borrows a fraction of the clock cycle time from a subsequent faster logic stage to meet timing requirements .
  • Why Latches: Latches are level-sensitive (e.g., transparent while Clock is HIGH). If data arrives slightly after the clock rises, the latch simply passes it through, effectively borrowing time from the transparency window. Flip-flops are edge-triggered and present a hard, inflexible timing boundary, capturing data only at a precise instant, making borrowing impossible .
Q: What is Metastability? Why does it happen, and where are Synchronizers used?
Answer:
  • Metastability: An unstable state where a flip-flop's output voltage hovers between Logic 0 and Logic 1 for an unbounded amount of time before randomly resolving .
  • Why it happens: It occurs when a signal violates the Setup or Hold time windows around the clock edge, failing to give the internal feedback loop time to resolve to a stable state .
  • Synchronizers: Used primarily in Clock Domain Crossing (CDC) (transferring data between asynchronous clocks) or for external asynchronous inputs. A standard 2-flop synchronizer gives the signal a full extra clock cycle to resolve out of metastability before entering the downstream logic .
Q: How many latches are in one flip-flop?
Answer: A standard edge-triggered flip-flop contains exactly two latches connected in series: a Master latch (transparent on one clock phase) and a Slave latch (transparent on the opposite phase) .
Q: What is the DFT stage? Why do we need to test the chip after manufacturing, and how is it done?
Answer:
  • DFT (Design For Testability): The process of adding extra logic structures to the RTL/netlist specifically to make testing the fabricated silicon easier .
  • Why test after manufacturing: Silicon fabrication is imperfect. Dust, lithography errors, or processing defects can cause physical shorts or opens (Stuck-at-0, Stuck-at-1 faults). This is completely separate from functional bugs .
  • How it checks the chip: Functional vectors alone cannot provide high fault coverage. DFT replaces standard flops with Scan Flops, chaining them together into huge shift registers. This allows ATE (Automated Test Equipment) to shift in arbitrary test vectors, capture the combinational logic response, and shift it out for comparison. BIST (Built-In Self-Test) generates random patterns on-chip to test memory arrays (MBIST) or logic (LBIST) automatically .
Q: What is Sign-off, and what checks are performed other than STA?
Answer: Sign-off is the absolute final verification phase where the layout and timing are rigorously checked against foundry rules before generating the GDSII file for tape-out .
Other than STA, checks include:
  • DRC (Design Rule Check): Ensures the layout meets manufacturing spacing/width rules.
  • LVS (Layout vs. Schematic): Verifies the physical layout matches the intended logical netlist.
  • IR Drop / EM Analysis (Power Sign-off): Checks voltage drop across the power grid and ensures wires won't melt due to electromigration.
Q: What is a K-map? What does 'K' refer to, and why is it used?
Answer: The 'K' stands for Karnaugh Map. It is a visual/graphical method used to simplify and minimize Boolean algebraic expressions without needing complex mathematical theorems. It organizes truth table inputs into a grid based on Gray code, allowing designers to group 1s and logically eliminate redundant variables .
Q: What are Combinational and Sequential circuits? Give examples.
Answer:
  • Combinational Circuits: The output depends only on the present state of the inputs. There is no memory or feedback. Example: Adders, Multiplexers, Decoders, ALU .
  • Sequential Circuits: The output depends on the present inputs and the past history (previous states). They contain memory elements and clocking mechanisms. Example: Flip-flops, Registers, Counters, Finite State Machines (FSMs) .
